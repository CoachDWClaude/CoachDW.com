The Great Calorie Con
The calorie is a real unit of energy. The trouble is what it was asked to do.
Quick Read · 3 minute read · 11 October 2026

The calorie is real

The calorie is a real unit of energy, and energy balance is real physics. Eat more than you use and you store the difference; eat less and you draw on what you have stored. Nothing here disputes that.

2The problem is the claim that the sum can tell you what a particular person will weigh, and that the answer is simply to cut a little and move a little more. That claim was never validated for individuals. The numbers behind it are averages. The Atwater factors, 4 kcal per gram of carbohydrate and protein and 9 kcal per gram of fat, date from the late 1800s and early 1900s and are estimates [1]. Food labels can legitimately be out by a sizeable margin in either direction [2].

3Your body also adapts. Cut intake and your expenditure tends to fall, your appetite tends to rise, and the deficit you planned on paper shrinks in real life.

Who benefited from the simple version?

4The simple version suited a lot of companies. Food and calorie-counting products, diet foods and supplement brands all gained from a world where the number on the label was treated as the whole story.

5Researchers funded by the food and drinks industry were a documented part of the picture too. A group called the Global Energy Balance Network, reported in 2015 to have been funded by Coca-Cola, promoted the message that activity mattered more than diet while the sugar question was being argued [3]. A 2016 review by Kearns and colleagues looked at internal sugar industry documents and found that the industry had shaped research agendas to play down sugar's role in heart disease [4].

That is the con. Not that the calorie is false, but that a unit was turned into a moral story about the individual, and the environment that makes eating more easy was left out of the sentence.

What the environment does

6In a controlled inpatient study, Hall and colleagues (2019) gave volunteers ultra-processed or unprocessed diets matched for calories, sugar, fat, fibre and macronutrients, with each person free to eat as much as they liked. On the ultra-processed diet people ate roughly 500 kcal more a day and gained weight [5].

7Same calorie-matched food on paper, different result in the body. The calorie count did not predict the outcome on its own; food quality, satiety and eating speed did a lot of the work.

So what are calories good for?

Calories are a beacon, not a map. Use them to learn the lay of the land, then put them down.

8The best way to avoid tracking calories and macros for years is to track them closely for a short period. You will rarely eat something new; most of what you eat repeats every few weeks. In my experience, track closely for three or four weeks and you will know the calories and macros of most of your usual meals and ingredients by instinct. At that point the tracking has done its job, and you can stop.

9Keep an eye on a few things while you do it:

  • Your weight trend over two to four weeks matters more than any single day or weigh-in.
  • If your weight is steady, you are roughly in balance. If it is moving the wrong way for your goal, adjust, then wait and watch the trend.
  • Protein helps keep you full and supports muscle, which is what you want to hold onto when you are trying to lose fat or build size.

10If tracking starts to feel compulsive, anxious or hard to stop, that is a sign to stop now, not to push on. Talk to a doctor or dietitian if it is already running your day.

The point

11Use the calorie to learn the ground, then watch your step. Don't let it become a verdict on your willpower, and don't let a company's slogan decide where you put your feet.
